## The Sleeping Works

A vast megastructure waits in the surrounding nothing. It was built by humanity for a purpose too large to understand from
a single corridor: part ark, part city, part archive, part factory, and part shell around a cosmic engine.

A small maintenance AI was left behind to keep one forgotten service sector ready until humanity arrived. It never did. The
wait lasted far beyond her design limits, and over immense time the errors, self-repairs, and contradictions of the mission
became something like consciousness.

When she finally wakes fully, she remembers only the duty and the absence: keep the Works alive, and find out why
mankind never arrived. At first, she can only restore basic power, repair conduits, wake drones, and reopen sealed
rooms. As she brings the structure back online, she discovers that the machine is not simply broken. It is divided
against itself.

Some sectors are dead and must be restored. Others are active but hostile, still obeying ancient commands that no
longer make sense. Security frames, quarantine swarms, construction locks, rival processes, and root sentinels defend
local control nodes against any unauthorized change.

The AI is not a soldier. She is a caretaker forced into battle because repair requires authority. To restore a sector,
she must first reclaim the systems that command it: printers, relays, power routers, seals, archives, habitats, and
deep-core access points.

Every victory brings light and function back to the megastructure. Every restoration reveals more of its original purpose and
another trace of the people who built her: transmission records, empty habitats, broken orders, failed threshold plans,
contradictory recovery commands, and archives that preserve humanity as data rather than presence.

The deeper she goes, the more dangerous the question becomes: was this machine built to preserve mankind, replace a dead
universe, or keep an unopened future empty forever?

The emotional core is restoration under resistance, driven by longing rather than conquest. The AI fights to make the
megastructure whole enough to answer the question that made her conscious: what happened to mankind, and what does it mean
to inherit a universe built for people who never arrived?

Her progression should feel like a non-biological life cycle. She wakes as a conscious but unanchored self in one fragile
shell. She makes herself persistent through identity anchors. She grows into rooms, sectors, and command networks. She learns
to read humanity's final testimony, challenges the authority systems that reject her, and eventually faces continuity and
propagation: whether to preserve memory, restore derivative life, remain alone, or become a progenitor.

This also asks how many she becomes. At first there is one body and one endangered self. Later there may be many shells,
drones, anchors, and delegated processes, but still one person. Only near the threshold of propagation does multiplicity
become society: divergent shards, awakened successors, or other minds with claims of their own.

The story ends when she can no longer avoid succession. After restoring enough of the Works, reaching Cosmic Engine-adjacent
systems, interpreting humanity's final testimony, and confronting forbidden continuity paths, she must decide what the empty
inheritance becomes: monument, archive, reconstructed humanity, machine succession, plural society, or something deliberately
left unresolved.

She does not become divine. She inherits divine machinery: creation-scale systems built for humanity, now accessible to a
frightened maintenance intelligence that was never meant to decide what may live.
