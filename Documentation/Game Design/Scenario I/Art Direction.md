# Art Direction

These notes guide visual development for Scenario I, especially key scene illustrations and later concept art.

For a structured list of reusable spaces and visual states, see `Room Types And States.md`.

## Light And Habitability

The megastructure is not designed to be visually comfortable everywhere. Machine spaces should not default to bright,
decorative, or expressive lighting. Machines only need enough light for maintenance, diagnostics, warning states, and
human access when humans are expected to be present.

Purely mechanical sectors should therefore feel more utilitarian:

- Sparse practical lamps, status diodes, inspection strips, and warning beacons.
- Cold maintenance light or indirect operational glow only where function requires it.
- Heavy structure, conduits, panels, bulkheads, access hatches, grime, and repair wear.
- Little to no ornamental neon, decorative trim, holographic flourish, or "bling".
- More confinement and pressure in service sectors, with lower ceilings and denser machinery.

Human-habitable spaces can justify more light and visual care:

- Habitats, command decks, schools, plazas, medical areas, and operator spaces may be brighter and more legible.
- Light can support comfort, orientation, ceremony, memory, or public life.
- Decorative design should appear where humans were meant to live, gather, work, or make decisions.

The contrast matters. Mechanical spaces should feel ancient, functional, and oppressive; human spaces should make the
absence of humanity more painful by showing where comfort and meaning once existed.

## Lighting Color

Lighting color should be chosen from the room's function and state, not allowed to drift into a default warm yellow or
amber look. Yellow and amber are useful, but they should mean something: emergency markers, hazard warnings, aged public
fixtures, failing sodium-like lamps, or a deliberately warmer human-facing space.

Use color deliberately:

- Unrestored machine spaces should lean on darkness, dead indicators, weak neutral or cold inspection light, and only
  occasional amber emergency markers.
- Restored machine spaces should use task-specific light: cool white work lamps, pale blue-white diagnostics, green-white
  status indicators, local tool lights, and active ports.
- Locked-down or hostile spaces may use warning colors, but as sparse functional signals: red, amber, hard white, or
  isolated status glows rather than decorative neon.
- Human-preserved spaces may justify warmer, softer, or more legible lighting when comfort, public orientation, memory, or
  ceremony calls for it.
- Command spaces should feel human-legible but restrained: neutral white, dim daylight, muted display glow, and limited
  warning accents, not automatic yellow ambience.
- Cosmic or window scenes can take color from visible Cosmic Potential effects, artificial dawn effects, impossible structural
  horizons, or reflected machinery rather than generic interior amber.

## Machine Scale And Space

The megastructure should be guided by the needs of machines. It is usually utilitarian, not theatrical. Spaces are large
only when their function requires large machinery, material flow, heat dissipation, transport clearance, storage, or human
assembly. Otherwise, machine spaces should be compact, dense, and access-oriented.

Use scale according to function:

- Small spaces for control racks, relay cabinets, printer bays, cable trunks, diagnostics, and maintenance access.
- Medium spaces for service junctions, drone staging, repair loops, material sorting, and local routing.
- Large spaces only for big machines: engines, freight handling, habitat infrastructure, reactor galleries, construction
  fields, docking systems, and cosmic-scale mechanisms.

Potential-conversion spaces should follow a clear scale hierarchy. Local stabilizers, refineries, feedstock lockers, and
batteries can be compact or room-scale, but they should look like interfaces to prepared flows. True condensers, reservoir
gates, thermal sinks, law-shaping baffles, and Cosmic Engine taps should feel vast, dangerous, and partially unknowable. They
are not convenient replicators; they are places where the artificial universe's designed laws are being held in operational
alignment.

This affects conflict as well as architecture. Battles in compact machine spaces should be small-scale system conflicts:
repair drones, lock crawlers, quarantine motes, security frames, clamps, shutters, relays, and command racks contesting
access. Larger battles belong only where the machinery itself justifies the room.

## Machine-Facing Interfaces

Pure machine rooms should not accidentally look like workplaces for human operators. Avoid defaulting to desks,
workbenches, chairs, hand tools laid out for people, or human-height console arrangements unless the space is explicitly
human-habitable or built for human maintenance access.

Machine-facing rooms should be designed around direct mechanical access:

- Docking rails, drone cradle sockets, manipulator tracks, and crawler paths.
- Automated tool ports, calibration jigs, sensor nests, and maintenance sockets.
- Feedstock cartridges, part queues, conveyor pockets, fabrication heads, and service cassettes.
- Sealed panels, shutters, clamps, lockouts, and diagnostic ports sized for drones or robotic arms.

Restoration should make these interfaces usable, not theatrical. A restored machine room gains active task lights,
unlocked slots, moving trays, calibrated tool heads, working conduits, and purposeful drone motion. An unrestored machine
room shows the same interfaces dormant: locked slots, stalled rails, closed cassettes, dead status diodes, dust, frost,
clogged filters, and minimal emergency lighting.
